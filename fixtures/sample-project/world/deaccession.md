The civic procedure by which something is deliberately forgotten, and the reason the
catalogues of [The Lantern Archive](./archive.md) cannot be trusted to agree with
themselves from one week to the next.

## The first principle: destruction is honest

Burning a book destroys the object and leaves the record. The Archive holds a complete
index of every volume that has ever burned — titles, authors, the dates and causes of
their loss. Fire is a civic tragedy and the Archive mourns it in writing.

Deaccession destroys the record, and the object follows the record down. There is no
entry, no gap where an entry was, no mourning. The shelf closes over the space like
water.

This is why the burned-book catalogue matters: it is the honest ledger, and its
completeness is the measure against which absence becomes visible. A book that is
missing from *both* the collection and the burn index was never lost. It was taken.

## What it costs, and who can do it

A thing cannot be forgotten by one person wanting it gone. Deaccession requires quorum —
a sufficient number of people agreeing, deliberately and in the same hour, that a thing
should never have been. This is what makes it a civic instrument rather than a private
crime, and it is why the power sits with a standing committee rather than with anyone
who simply owns a match.

*(The body that holds this authority is deliberately thin here — it belongs to the
factions pass.)*

The cut is never clean. Excising a record takes tissue from whatever the record touched:
a name goes and the street named for it goes shabby; a book goes and the three books that
cited it develop gaps their authors cannot account for. This surrounding damage is a
**wake**, and wakes are the only honest evidence Deaccession leaves behind.

## Where the forgotten goes

Beneath the Archive there is a river, and the river is sealed.

Forgotten things do not stop existing. They go down. The river is the sink the city has
been pouring its unwanted history into for as long as there has been a committee to pour
it, and it was sealed — bricked, gated, struck from the civic maps — because what was in
it began to come back up.

The seal is holding. It is not holding well.

## Tells

Deaccession is silent by design, but the river is not.

- **Water misbehaves.** Rain runs uphill. Dry stone sweats. Wells rise in drought. The
  closer to the Archive steps, the worse it reads. Water is the medium the river pushes
  back through, and it is the most reliable warning the city has and refuses to use.
- **Metal runs warm.** Brass especially, and most of all brass that sits close to the
  seal. A fitting that should be cold to the palm is a fitting with something behind it.
- **Seams in the catalogue.** An entry whose neighbours do not line up. Shelf-marks that
  skip. Spacing that is too even, as though the column were reset. The catalogue heals
  over a Deaccession, but it heals like skin — smooth, and slightly wrong.

## Who can perceive it

Neither of your leads can see the whole thing, which is the point.

**Mara Vale** reads seams. She perceives *that* something was removed — the reset column,
the shelf-mark with no shelf — and never what. Her evidence is absence: unarguable to her,
and impossible to hand to anyone else.

**Oren Pike** retains content. Anyone standing inside a wake when it passes — physically
in the Archive, or bound closely enough to the excised thing — keeps fragments the record
no longer supports. But retention is not proof, and the world reorganises to make the
rememberer wrong: the citations fail, the witnesses shrug, the paper trail declines to
exist. A man who insists on a book that was never written is not a witness. He is a
disgraced historian.

So he has content without credibility, and she has proof without content. Separately they
are dismissable. Together they are a problem, and that is the shape of the mystery.
